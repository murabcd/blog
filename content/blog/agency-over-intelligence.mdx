## Waiting for Someone to Decide

A team can understand a problem perfectly and still leave it unresolved. Everyone can explain the tradeoffs, but nobody makes the next call, talks to the customer, or takes responsibility for the result. After enough meetings, the analysis becomes a way to postpone ownership.

After years of building and leading product teams, I think we overvalue raw intelligence. A team can be full of smart people and still analyze a problem indefinitely. I would put more weight on the person who takes that understanding, makes a sensible decision, and stays responsible for what happens next.

## Agency Turns Analysis into Work

Agency means taking initiative and owning an outcome within the constraints of the team. Someone notices a problem, finds the people who understand it, proposes a way forward, and follows through. They ask for help when needed without treating every missing instruction as a reason to stop.

That ownership includes alignment. The person needs to understand the team's goals and know which decisions they can make. Once those boundaries are clear, they should be able to move without waiting for someone to prepare every step.

## What Ownership Looks like in Practice

A product manager receives a feature request from sales. Before turning it into a ticket, they ask which customer workflow is failing, speak to the people involved, and check whether the proposed feature would solve it. Their contribution is making the problem clear enough for the team to choose a response.

An engineer spots a constraint in the proposed implementation. They explain the failure mode, suggest an alternative, and make the cost visible while there is still time to change direction. Replacing the entire architecture without discussion would create a different problem.

A designer sees a gap in the user flow and builds a prototype to test the assumption. They bring the result back to the team before anyone invests in detailed screens. In each case, ownership includes producing evidence that helps other people act.

## Hire for Evidence of Initiative

Well-defined interview exercises tell you something about how a person solves a problem. They tell you less about whether that person will notice an important problem and take responsibility for it.

I would spend time on work the candidate initiated. Ask what they saw, what authority they had, who disagreed, and what happened after the first attempt failed. Small examples can be more revealing than a large project whose direction was already decided by someone else.

Then look at the environment you are offering them. If every imperfect decision gets punished, people learn to ask permission for everything. Hiring for initiative achieves little when the organization rewards waiting.

## Agency Is the Bottleneck

Intelligence creates options. Agency turns one of them into action. A team can know exactly what is wrong and still wait for permission, a cleaner brief, or somebody else to own the problem.

That makes agency something you have to hire for and protect. If initiative is punished whenever it produces an imperfect result, people learn to wait. Eventually the organization knows more than it can act on.
