## The Myth of the Blitzscale

The dominant narrative in tech is one of relentless speed. We're told to launch quickly, fail fast, and blitzscale our way to market leadership. The prevailing wisdom is that growth at all costs is the only way to win.

But after years of observing what makes products truly last, I've come to believe this is a dangerous myth. A narrow initial audience can give a team enough contact with the work to make the product useful. Speed still matters, especially while funding is limited, but growth can amplify an unclear product before the team knows why anyone would keep using it.

## Build for Yourself, Then Find Your Tribe

The most common mistake I see is teams trying to build a product for "everyone." A product designed for everyone is, almost by definition, a product that is exceptional for no one. It is an exercise in compromise and mediocrity.

The most powerful product visions are born from a place of deep personal conviction. The founders of a now-dominant project management tool weren't trying to solve a hypothetical problem for a generic user persona. They were frustrated designers and engineers at top tech companies, and they set out to build a tool that *they* would love to use. They were their own ideal customer.

This is a critical starting point. When you are building for yourself, you have an intuitive, almost unfair advantage. You understand the nuances of the workflow, you feel the pain of the existing solutions, and you have an uncompromisingly high bar for quality. Your daily experience provides a starting hypothesis. Other customers may have different permissions, incentives, budgets, and workflows, so that familiarity still needs testing.

The next step is to validate that you are not alone. The founding team of that project management tool spent over a year running informal, unstructured user research with their peers—other high-performing individual contributors at fast-growing tech companies. They used those conversations to investigate whether peers experienced the same frustration and how they handled it. This pre-work gave them the conviction that their personal problem was also a market problem.

## The Power of the Velvet Rope

Once you have this conviction, the temptation is to open the floodgates and let everyone in. The unscalable approach does the opposite. Instead of a public launch that attracts a wide and varied audience, you create a private beta with a velvet rope.

A waitlist can collect the context needed to select a useful beta cohort. To get an invite, potential users should be asked to explain why they want to use the product and what problems they are facing with their current tools.

This allows you to hand-pick your first users. Select a small cohort with a clear need and enough motivation to test an unfinished product. Keep their selection criteria visible so the team remembers which parts of the market the feedback represents. By onboarding them in small, controlled groups, you gain two powerful benefits:

1.  **High-Signal Feedback:** Your feedback comes from users who are deeply invested and perfectly match your ideal customer profile. Their feedback is easier to interpret because you understand why they joined and what they are trying to do. It can still be biased toward people willing to tolerate an unfinished product.
2.  **Reputation Management:** You protect your product's reputation by preventing "bad-fit" users from having a negative first experience and then dismissing your product forever.

## A Framework for Your Early Roadmap

With this curated group of early users, your roadmap becomes incredibly clear. The work falls into two simple categories:

-   **Enablers:** These are the features that will make your existing, highly motivated users ecstatically happy. They are the enhancements that deepen the value for your core audience and turn them into evangelists. In the early days, this is where you should spend most of your energy.
-   **Blockers:** These are the features that prevent other "good-fit" customers from adopting your product. A common early blocker for a B2B SaaS tool might be the lack of a specific single sign-on (SSO) integration, which excludes qualified customers who operate within a different identity provider's ecosystem.

A disciplined early-stage roadmap is a constant balancing act between delighting your core users with enablers and methodically removing blockers to expand your addressable market.

## Start Narrow

Choose a cohort whose problem you can describe precisely, then watch whether they return without repeated persuasion. Ask what still blocks the work and which improvement would deepen the value they already receive.

Use that evidence to decide when to broaden access. A small beta should reduce uncertainty, not become a way to postpone testing whether the product can support a wider business.
