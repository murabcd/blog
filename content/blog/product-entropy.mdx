## More Is No Longer the Constraint

One of the biggest shifts in software right now is that adding something new is no longer the hard part. Teams can move from idea to prototype with very little friction. They can test more paths, try more variations, and push much further before they feel the weight that implementation used to impose.

That sounds like pure progress, and in many ways it is. Faster execution is genuinely useful. It helps teams learn sooner. It lowers the cost of exploring. It gives product, design, and engineering more room to test their thinking before they commit too deeply.

But it also removes a filter.

## The Product Gets Heavier in Quiet Ways

A feature starts creating obligations as soon as someone depends on it. The initial implementation estimate covers only part of that cost.

Once something ships, it rarely stays isolated. It begins to touch onboarding, support, documentation, QA, analytics, edge cases, and future roadmap decisions. It becomes one more thing users can misunderstand, one more thing the team has to preserve, and one more branch every future decision has to route around.

This weight does not arrive all at once. That is why teams underestimate it. The product becomes a little harder to navigate, a little harder to explain, and a little harder to change. Then it happens again. And again. That is how entropy enters the product.

## Bad Features Are Not the Main Threat

A clear failure can make the next decision easier. The team can inspect what happened, remove the experiment, and carry the lesson into another attempt. Ambiguous results are harder to act on because someone can usually find a reason to keep the feature.

The real problem is everything that lands in the middle. Features that are not important enough to define the product, but not useless enough to be deleted. They remain because someone uses them, because removing them creates friction, or because nobody feels enough pain to challenge them.

These are the features that slowly change the character of a product. They do not usually break it in one moment. They dilute it over time.

## AI Increases the Need for Editorial Judgment

In the past, the cost of building acted as a form of discipline. Teams had to argue harder for what deserved to exist because implementation was expensive enough to force prioritization.

That discipline is weakening. Agents can generate code, wire flows together, and turn vague ideas into working artifacts quickly. This is useful for exploration, but it also makes accumulation easier. When the distance between idea and implementation collapses, weak ideas make it further into the system.

Review what the feature does to the rest of the product. A working implementation is one input to that decision; the customer's mental model and the team's future obligations matter too.

Does it sharpen the product's value, or blur it? Does it remove complexity somewhere else, or only add new complexity of its own? Does it strengthen the product's identity, or just increase its volume?

Those are editorial questions. And they matter more now than ever.

## Strong Products Exclude More than They Include

A clear product boundary helps the team reject additions that would pull existing workflows in conflicting directions.

This is why lower implementation cost should not lead to lower standards for what gets absorbed into the product. Keep experiments separate from supported product commitments until the team has enough evidence to accept their recurring cost. Teams should absolutely test more ideas. They should prototype aggressively. They should learn faster than before. But the core product still needs a gate.

Otherwise every cheap experiment leaves behind residue.

## Protect Clarity

Cheap implementation gives teams more room to try ideas. It also makes it easier for an experiment to become a permanent obligation without anyone making a deliberate choice to keep it. The product starts carrying the consequences long after the prototype felt inexpensive.

That is where product judgment matters. Trying more can help the team learn. Letting every plausible addition stay gives those experiments control over the product's direction. Clarity has to be protected through the decisions that follow the build.
