## Listening Too Closely

There is a familiar playbook in product: find your happiest customers, ask them what they want, and give it to them. These people know the product, explain their requests clearly, and may be willing to pay. Their feedback is much easier to act on than a conversation with someone who never made it through setup.

That convenience creates a selection problem. The people who succeeded with the product tell you how to make it better for them. If they supply nearly all the roadmap input, the obstacles that kept other customers out can remain invisible.

## Building for the Expert

A superuser has already learned the terminology, configured the workflow, and found the useful shortcuts. More fields, automation rules, and customization can make their work faster. A new customer encounters those same capabilities before understanding what they are for.

Imagine a project management product shaped around one experienced team's complex workflow. The team asks for another approval rule, and the rule is useful. But every new team now has another decision to understand before creating its first project. Repeating that tradeoff can improve expert use while making adoption harder.

The issue is who pays for the additional capability. The requesting customer sees the time saved; the newcomer sees the setup required. A roadmap that records only the first perspective misses part of the cost.

## Short-Term Profit

An expansion deal makes the expert's request concrete. The revenue has an owner, an amount, and a deadline. Improving first use has a less certain return, especially when the people who abandoned setup are difficult to reach.

That difference can bias the investment decision without anyone choosing to neglect growth. Each quarter, the visible account receives another improvement while the entry path waits. The company can become very good at expanding relationships with customers who already understand it and increasingly poor at starting new ones.

## The Entry Path Gets Harder

A new buyer needs to recognize the problem, understand the product's approach, and reach a useful result. If the first encounter exposes years of expert configuration, they have to invest in learning before knowing whether that investment will pay off.

Some products genuinely need that depth. The decision is how much of it a newcomer must confront immediately. Defaults, progressive disclosure, and a narrower initial workflow can protect access to the core value without removing the capabilities experienced users rely on.

## The Echo Chamber

Research can reinforce the same bias. The most engaged users answer invitations, attend advisory calls, and explain their requests in the team's own language. Their feedback starts to feel representative because it is detailed and familiar.

Include people who struggled to activate, chose an alternative, or needed substantial help to begin. Their experience may reveal a different problem from the one the advisory group wants solved. Keep those groups distinct when deciding what the evidence means.

## Build for the Next Customer

Your best customers can explain what makes the product worth staying with. They have less to tell you about the barriers they crossed years ago or the ones their own requests have since created.

Keep their depth while protecting a way in for the next customer. Otherwise the product can get better for the people who already understand it while becoming harder for anyone else to choose.
