## The Discount before the Diagnosis

A deal slows down, so the seller offers a discount. Sometimes price is the obstacle. But if the buyer cannot explain why the product matters, a lower price leaves the main problem unresolved. It can also make them wonder why the original price was so flexible.

This is why I think selling starts with diagnosis. You need to understand the work the customer is trying to do, the cost of the current approach, and the reason they might change it now. Without that, the pitch becomes a sequence of attempts to produce a yes.

## The Best Sales Feel like Service

A useful expert asks questions before proposing an answer. They clarify the problem, explain what they can address, and make the limits visible. A buyer can leave that conversation with a better understanding of their situation even if they decide against the product.

That kind of sale builds trust because the recommendation is tied to evidence. The seller can connect a feature to a specific workflow and explain why the expected benefit is worth the effort. Payment still matters, as do budget and procurement; understanding the problem makes those discussions more concrete.

## Write the Playbook before Scaling It

An experienced sales leader from a mature company may know how to manage territories, quotas, and an established pipeline. An early-stage business also needs to discover why people buy, what objections mean, and which customers can succeed with the product.

Those are different kinds of work. A leader can have experience in both, but a recognizable company name on a resume does not prove it. Hire against the uncertainty you actually need someone to resolve.

Founders and product teams should stay close to that discovery. If the pitch keeps failing at the same point, more sales activity may reproduce the same misunderstanding at a higher cost.

## Learn from Specific Customer Work

Custom integrations and messy customer problems can teach a product company how a market operates. A paid project creates access to constraints that a generic interview may never surface. That learning can inform reusable product capabilities.

It can also become an expensive distraction. Before accepting the work, decide what is reusable, who owns delivery, and which ongoing commitments the project creates. A deep integration can improve retention when it supports an important workflow; installation effort alone does not guarantee value or loyalty.

I would judge custom work by what the company learns and can reuse after the customer is live. Revenue is useful evidence, but so are the maintenance obligations that come with it.

## Selling Starts with Helping

A useful sale leaves both sides better off. The customer gets help with a problem worth solving, and the seller gets paid for providing it. Pressure to hit a number can make people lose sight of that exchange.

I think the sales process becomes easier to trust when the seller stays willing to recommend a smaller purchase, a different approach, or no purchase at all. Helping the customer choose well is part of the work, including when the answer costs you a deal.
