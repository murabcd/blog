## A Different Job Filter

If I were looking for a job today, I would ask a question that barely existed a few years ago: how much are the people here actually working with agents?

I would want to know if the strongest people are agent maxxing: running several agents, delegating real work, building their own loops, comparing workflows, and spending enough tokens that nobody is nervously watching the meter. An AI strategy or a company-wide subscription tells me very little about that.

That would matter more to me than a lot of the usual signals. You are partly choosing a company, but you are also choosing the environment in which you will learn how work gets done next.

## You Need Repetitions

Working through real tasks gives you failure cases a prompt guide or polished demo may omit. You give an agent a task, watch it misunderstand the shape of the problem, figure out which context was missing, change the way you delegate, and try again.

Over time you start noticing where agents need freedom and where they need constraints. You learn when to keep one thread going, when to start over, when to run work in parallel, and when the output looks plausible but has not touched the real problem. None of this becomes instinct after ten prompts.

The people getting unusually good at agents are compressing hundreds of these loops into their normal work. They encounter more failures, try more ways through them, and bring that experience into the next task.

## Unlimited Tokens Change Behavior

This is why effectively unlimited tokens change how willing you are to practice. They give you room to compare alternatives and discard an attempt that taught you something.

When usage is tightly rationed, every interaction starts to feel precious. People save agents for work that looks important enough. They avoid parallel attempts. They accept mediocre output because another pass feels wasteful. They use the tool carefully when the useful behavior is often to use it aggressively, inspect what happened, and run the loop again.

That caution costs the team experiments as well as tokens. Someone with effectively unlimited access can try three approaches, give the same task to different agents, let one investigate while another implements, and throw away work without treating it as a failure. That person will develop judgment faster because they see more outcomes.

## The People around You Set the Ceiling

Access alone is not enough. A company can pay for unlimited usage and still have nobody worth learning from.

I would want to work with people who are already discovering strange, useful ways to use agents. People who can show me why they split a task a certain way, how they structure a repository for agents, what they automate, what they always review themselves, and which workflows they abandoned after trying them seriously.

That kind of environment creates a shared learning loop. One person finds a better way to run research. Another figures out how to keep long tasks verifiable. Someone else turns a repeated conversation into an automation. The techniques spread through actual work instead of arriving as another internal AI workshop.

The opposite environment is expensive even if the salary is good. If everyone around you treats agents as autocomplete, you will probably do the same. Your baseline will be set by people who are barely using the tools, and you will have to discover every useful pattern alone.

## Optimize for the Learning Rate

There is still plenty of uncertainty about what agent-heavy work will look like. That is exactly why I would optimize for exposure now.

The transferable skill is quickly understanding what a new agent is good at, building a working loop around it, and telling when the result can be trusted. That judgment transfers when the models and interfaces change.

You cannot build it from the sidelines. You need real work, failed delegation, a lot of tokens, and people around you who are pushing the tools harder than you are. If I were choosing my next job, I would choose the place that gives me the most repetitions.
