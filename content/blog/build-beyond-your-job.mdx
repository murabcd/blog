## What Leaves With You

Someone can spend years becoming essential inside a company and leave with surprisingly little they can show outside it. They know how decisions get approved, who needs convincing, and which internal problems keep returning. That knowledge helps them do the job, but much of its value depends on staying in the same environment.

I think about this when I read advice about surviving AI. Learning the tools matters. But if more of the work inside a role becomes easier to automate, being excellent at its current task list may offer less protection than we expect. I would also want to know what I am building that remains useful when the role changes.

That could be an understanding of a problem, people who trust my judgment, work others can inspect, or something I own and can keep developing. A good job can help build all of these. It can also keep someone busy enough to build none of them.

## Get Closer to the Problem

If everyone has access to similar tools, the differences in what we notice become more valuable. Two people can ask the same model for a product idea. The person who has watched a customer struggle through the workflow has something better to start with.

For someone building support software, that might mean spending time with the team handling escalations. Watch what happens when the customer has already contacted three departments, the account history is incomplete, and nobody agrees on who owns the case. There are product decisions in that situation that a clean process diagram will never expose.

Experiences outside work matter too. Organizing an event, making something physical, or helping someone run a small business puts you in contact with constraints you cannot edit away in a document. Materials arrive late. People misunderstand instructions. Someone has to pay for the mistake. You come back with different questions because you have seen a different kind of failure.

The useful habit is to capture what surprised you: what you expected, what happened, and which assumption broke. Over time, those observations give you something specific to contribute. Reading the same feeds as everyone else rarely does.

## Make Your Work Useful to Others

Relationships become stronger through doing things together. Help someone finish a difficult project. Make an introduction that solves a problem. Organize a small gathering where people can compare work honestly. Spend time around people whose standards make you reconsider your own.

Mentoring belongs here too. Explaining a decision to someone earlier in their career exposes gaps in your own reasoning. Helping them become more capable also changes what you can build together later. I would rather be part of a group that keeps getting stronger than compete to remain the person everyone depends on.

Sharing work makes these relationships possible beyond the people you already know. A working tool, a clear article, or a useful explanation gives someone a reason to start a conversation. Show the problem, what you tried, and where the result still falls short. That is more useful than announcing that you are interested in AI.

Selling is part of this. People need to understand why something deserves their time before they try it. A narrow interest can help: you may care far more than most people about a particular workflow or an awkward problem. Explain it well enough and the other people who care can find you.

## Keep Something You Can Build On

There is a difference between finishing an assignment and creating something that remains useful afterward. A tool others can extend, a body of writing, or a small business can carry work forward across changes in employment. Each also creates responsibilities. Tools need maintenance, readers need a reason to return, and customers expect you to keep showing up.

I find that ownership question more useful than trying to identify the one skill AI will never touch. What can I improve over several years? Who can use it without asking for my time again? What am I learning from maintaining it that the next project can benefit from?

This also changes how I judge the work filling a calendar. Some coordination is necessary. But becoming better at navigating an approval chain can consume the same time I could spend understanding a customer, teaching someone, or building something useful. If I want institutional authority, I should pursue it deliberately. I would not want years of internal positioning to become my career plan by default.

None of this requires an accurate prediction about when a particular job changes. It gives me more ways to act when it does. I want to leave a role with people who trust my work, evidence I can show, and something useful I can keep building.
