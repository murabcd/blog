## The Investment Explosion

AI investment now reaches well beyond model training. OpenAI's [July 2025 Oracle announcement](https://openai.com/index/stargate-advances-with-partnership-with-oracle/) describes an agreement for 4.5 gigawatts of additional data center capacity and a broader $500 billion infrastructure commitment over four years. Those are development plans and commitments, rather than a measure of money already spent.

The scale makes me suspicious of the expectations built into it. Infrastructure takes years to develop, while the demand, pricing, and products expected to pay for it are still changing. A useful technology can attract investment at prices its eventual returns do not justify.

That is what I mean by a possible bubble. The question is who captures enough value to support the capital committed. Showing that AI is useful does not settle that question.

## When Models Become Commodities

A model lead is valuable when it lets a customer do work an alternative cannot. The business problem appears when competing models become good enough for the same workload. The buyer can then compare price, reliability, and integration cost instead of paying primarily for access to a unique capability.

That will happen at different speeds across tasks. A specialized reasoning or coding workload may still depend heavily on the model chosen. A routine classification task may offer much less room to charge for a small quality difference.

I would be cautious about building a business whose defense requires winning every next model comparison. The training bill returns, and the customer has another opportunity to switch.

## The Scramble for a Moat

Model companies have a reason to build advantages in distribution, infrastructure, and the applications people use every day. A customer who depends on the surrounding workflow has more to consider than which model topped the latest benchmark.

OpenAI's moves show the breadth of that effort: the infrastructure agreement sits alongside products such as [ChatGPT Atlas, announced in October 2025](https://openai.com/index/introducing-chatgpt-atlas/). I read that as an attempt to own more of the relationship with the user, rather than leaving the model as an interchangeable component inside someone else's product.

Owning more layers creates more opportunities to retain value. It also creates more products to maintain and more capital at risk. Expansion by itself does not prove the defense will hold.

## Value Moves to the Edges

If model access becomes easier to substitute for a workload, other constraints matter more. Down the stack, chips, energy, and data center capacity can be scarce. Their owners may gain pricing power, while still carrying utilization, financing, and construction risk. Selling infrastructure does not guarantee a return.

Up the stack, the advantage can come from the workflow: access to relevant records, distribution to the right users, and a product people can trust to complete the job. Those assets can remain valuable when the underlying model changes.

This is why I care about where a company sits in the task. Providing an answer is one role. Owning the process that turns it into completed work can be a much harder position for a competitor to replace.

## The Economics of Abundance

Cheaper execution can create demand for work that previously cost too much to attempt. A team might compare more campaign drafts or build a temporary internal tool for a narrow project. Some savings become additional output rather than a smaller bill.

That does not imply unlimited useful demand. Someone still has to choose the work, review the result, and maintain what survives. If those costs dominate, making generation cheaper may change less than the model provider expects.

The interesting product opportunity is to make a previously impractical task worth doing from start to finish. Generating more artifacts is only useful when the organization can turn them into a result.

## Software Starts to Disappear

An assistant might let someone complete a task without learning every screen in the underlying application. That could change which interfaces people visit and which companies own the customer relationship. The records, permissions, and business rules still have to live somewhere.

I think that is a more useful product bet than another general promise of intelligence. Own a task closely enough that the user can rely on its completion, even as models and interfaces change underneath it.

The infrastructure buildout can make more of those products possible. It cannot decide which ones deserve to exist, or whether enough of their value will reach the companies paying for it.
