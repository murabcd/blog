## Your Competitors Are Everywhere

You used to have a reasonably clear idea of who you competed with. They sold a similar product, targeted similar customers, and appeared in the same deals. You could put their logos on a slide and explain why you were different.

That slide is becoming less useful.

The company you integrate with can build your core feature. The agency implementing your product can turn its experience into software. Two people with coding agents can test an idea that used to require a funded team. Your customer can build enough of the workflow internally to question why they need you.

Your competitors now include people you used to treat as partners, suppliers, or customers. More people can act on the same opportunity, including people who had no practical way to enter your market a year ago. Anyone close enough to understand the problem has a better chance of building a business around solving it.

## Everyone Can Do More

AI expands what a team can attempt with the people and resources it already has. A company can improve its existing product faster, then use the same tools to move into work it previously left to someone else.

An analytics company can help customers take action on what it finds. An automation company can add the analysis that used to happen before its workflow started. A product that helped employees do a task can start doing parts of that task itself.

Each move looks reasonable from inside the company. Customers already ask for it. The team already has some of the data. Building a first version has become affordable.

From the outside, those reasonable expansions add up to more companies competing for the same work. Your market gets crowded even if nobody sets out to copy you.

The same opportunity that lets you expand makes it easier for everyone around you to expand toward you.

## Your Category Offers Less Protection

Imagine selling software that reviews support conversations and tells managers where their team needs coaching.

You could spend all your time comparing yourself with other conversation review products. Meanwhile, the help desk adds reviews, the training platform adds conversation analysis, and an automation vendor connects the two.

None of them needs to reproduce your entire product. They need to solve enough of the customer's problem to make a separate purchase unnecessary.

Customers rarely care about preserving the boundaries between software categories. They care about getting the work done with a reasonable amount of cost and effort. If a tool they already use can handle it, your more complete feature set has to justify another contract, integration, and habit.

A product can lead its category while the reason to buy that category gets weaker.

## Small Teams Can Become Relevant Quickly

There is also less time between someone noticing an opportunity and putting a credible alternative in front of a customer.

That does not mean every prototype becomes a business. Selling, reliability, implementation, and support still take work. But a small team can test demand before hiring the organization that would once have been necessary to build the first version.

This changes who deserves your attention. Team size, funding, and years in the market tell you less about what someone can put in front of your customers next month.

The same applies inside the customer's company. An employee who understands one awkward process can build a narrow tool around it. They do not need your permissions system, every integration, or a configurable interface. They only need the part their team uses.

You still have a business if buying removes enough work and responsibility. But the comparison now includes people who can solve a smaller problem on their own terms.

## Speed Stops Being a Comfortable Advantage

The obvious response is to move faster. That helps. A slow company gives everyone else more time to find its customers, learn its market, and close the gaps.

But faster tools are available to the other teams too. A feature that took you a week might take them a week. Shipping quickly can get you into a deal without giving you a durable reason to win it.

I would be careful about building a strategy around the assumption that competitors will stay busy with their current products. The next expansion might be obvious to them for exactly the same reason it is obvious to you: the customer wants the next step, and the team can now afford to build it.

That makes focus harder. There are more opportunities worth considering and more threats that look plausible. Chasing all of them leaves the customer with a product whose direction changes every time someone else ships.

## You Need to Know Why You Win

I think the practical response starts with a harder standard for differentiation.

What do you understand about the customer's work that makes your product substantially more useful? Which difficult cases can you handle reliably? How do customers find you? What responsibility can they hand over to you that an internal tool or a feature in another platform would leave with them?

Those answers need to survive a competitor gaining the capability you have today. A head start buys time to develop them. It does not replace them.

I would also keep widening the competitive conversation. Ask customers what they would use if your product disappeared. Look at which existing supplier could absorb the job. Pay attention when a customer starts building around a gap you assumed only a vendor could close.

Partners, platforms, small teams, and customers are all potential competitors now, and you are a potential competitor to more companies than before. That creates room to build something valuable. It also means every team is making its plans in a market where the other players can change what they do.

If your strategy requires them to stay in their lane, it is already fragile.
