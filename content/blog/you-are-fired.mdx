## The Hard Truth about Firing

Firing someone is one of the hardest decisions a leader makes. Once the decision is necessary, it is tempting to describe the employee as the problem and move on. That leaves management's part of the story unexamined.

Look at what the company hired for, what it actually asked the person to do, and what support and feedback they received. A mismatch can begin in hiring or develop after the role changes. Either way, there is something for leadership to understand.

## Resolve the Doubt

Repeated concern is a reason to investigate, not a decision by itself. Get specific about the expectations, the observed gap, and the feedback already given. Separate a performance problem from unclear ownership, inadequate support, or disagreement about how the work should be done.

Listen when colleagues raise concerns, then examine the evidence directly. The company owes the employee a clear account of the problem and a process appropriate to the situation. Postponing that work leaves both the team and the employee in uncertainty.

## Keep the Conversation Clear

Prepare with the people responsible for employment matters before holding the conversation. Notice, final pay, severance, and any review or appeal process depend on the jurisdiction, contract, and circumstances. A general management article cannot supply a universal termination script. For example, [Acas guidance for Great Britain](https://www.acas.org.uk/dismissals) emphasizes fair procedure, clear reasons, and confidentiality.

Once the decision has been reached through that process, explain it directly and respectfully. Give the actual reason, the effective timing, and the written next steps. Avoid hiding a performance decision behind an undefined claim about "culture fit."

Keep the meeting focused without demanding that the person show no emotion. Leave room for questions about what happens next. Any proposed agreement should follow the applicable process, with time for the person to understand it; threatening to remove an offer because they disagree turns a difficult conversation into pressure.

Serious misconduct needs investigation and appropriate action too. The seriousness of an allegation makes a fair process more important, rather than establishing an automatic rule about notice or severance.

## Tell the Team What Changes

Tell colleagues that the person has left and explain how responsibilities will be covered. Be clear about the operational changes without circulating private performance details. People need to know who owns the work; they do not need a public account of the departing employee's failures.

If the team has concerns, address what you can responsibly discuss. Respecting confidentiality still leaves room to explain expectations, ownership, and the support available to people doing the work.

## Fix Your Hiring Machine

Getting a bad fit out of your company is a massive relief. Now you need to make sure it does not happen again.

Stop and analyze what went wrong. Talk to the people who interviewed the fired employee. Ask them why the red flags were missed. 

Most importantly, look at the references. Did you actually check them? Use references appropriate to the role and ask about the specific work and conditions the person will face. A fixed count matters less than whether the conversation tests the assumptions behind the hire. Doing your research before hiring is just as critical as doing diligence before taking investor money. If the problems you experienced were hinted at by past employers, you failed to listen.

## Be Clear about References

If you cannot provide a useful reference, say so directly when appropriate. Silence is ambiguous and should not be treated as a reliable signal about a former employee. Follow the company's policy and keep any reference accurate and within what you can substantiate.

When you can recommend someone, make the recommendation specific. Explain the work you saw them do well and the environment in which they succeeded. That helps the next employer assess fit more than a general statement of enthusiasm.

## Review the Decision You Made

A departure can bring relief while leaving the original management problem intact. Review the hiring criteria, the expectations after joining, and the point at which the mismatch became visible. Find the decision the company could have made better.

The lesson needs to change something specific: how the role is defined, what the interview tests, how feedback arrives, or how a manager responds when the job changes. Otherwise the company can make the same mistake again with a different person.
