## The Screen Arrives Too Early

Ask an AI tool to design an onboarding flow and you can have a convincing interface before the team has agreed on what onboarding needs to accomplish. The screens have a clear hierarchy. The copy sounds reasonable. The buttons lead somewhere.

AI has made form generation cheap. A tool can produce screens, flows, and copy before the team has settled the decisions underneath them. This is useful, but generating form is not the same thing as design.

Who is getting stuck? What do they need to understand before they can continue? Which step protects the business, and which step exists because the old system required it? What happens when the information is incomplete?

The generated interface answers these questions whether you asked it to or not. It fills the gaps with familiar patterns and reasonable defaults. By the time the team sees the result, those assumptions already look like design decisions.

## Screens Can Hide Missing Decisions

This is the strange thing about modern design tools. They are getting much better at producing something that looks considered. That does not mean the underlying decisions have been considered.

Imagine a support product that needs a new escalation workflow. A generated interface might include a priority label, an owner field, a timeline, and a button to escalate the conversation. Every part makes sense in isolation.

The difficult questions begin after the screen looks complete. Can any employee escalate, or only a team lead? Does escalation transfer ownership or request help? What happens when two teams disagree about who should act? Can the customer see that the case changed status? When does the escalation expire?

Those decisions define the workflow. The interface only exposes them.

If the team has not worked through them, the consequences surface in production. People interpret the same status differently. The button triggers an action nobody fully owns. More labels and warnings appear to explain exceptions that should have changed the workflow itself.

The product does not fail because the interface lacks polish. It fails because the polish arrived before the understanding.

## Visual Work Is Part of the Thinking

This is why I still prefer designing visually over describing an interface in a prompt. Moving elements around gives me time to notice relationships I did not understand at the beginning.

If two actions keep competing for the same place, the problem may not be hierarchy. The product may be asking someone to make two different decisions at once. If a screen needs several explanations, the issue may not be the copy. The workflow may contain a concept the user should never have to manage.

That kind of understanding appears while working through the details. I change the sequence, remove something, try the uncomfortable case, and discover that my first version described the wrong problem. The visual work is not decoration after the thinking. It is one of the ways the thinking happens.

Writing works the same way for me. I often discover the argument by trying to explain it. The sentences expose gaps that felt invisible in my head. A finished paragraph is useful, but the more valuable result is the clearer thought that produced it.

When AI generates the finished-looking version too early, it can remove that part of the process. I receive an answer before I have created enough friction to understand the question.

## Use AI to Create Evidence

AI still belongs in design work. It can turn an uncertain idea into something people can use. It can generate several approaches, expose edge cases, and make a disagreement concrete enough to test.

But the goal should not be to accept the first convincing interface. The goal should be to learn what the interface reveals.

Give the prototype to someone who does not know how it was built. Watch where they hesitate. Try the incomplete account, the conflicting permission, and the case that changes halfway through. Compare alternatives that make different assumptions instead of asking the tool for more versions of the same one.

A useful prototype does not prove that the design is done. It gives the team better evidence for the next decision.

The tools can generate form faster than ever. That makes it easier to explore, but it also makes unfinished thinking look complete.

Form generation gives the team something to inspect. Design is the work of resolving what that form exposes.
