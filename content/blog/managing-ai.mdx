## It Just Doesn't Get It

"I have to repeat myself constantly." "It makes basic mistakes." "It just doesn't get what I'm asking for." People say these things about AI, and they sound a lot like someone complaining about a junior employee.

After integrating AI agents into my own teams and workflows, I've started paying more attention to that resemblance. A request can feel obvious while it is still in your head. Handing it over forces you to find out how much of the goal, context, and judgment you actually explained.

A model can fail a clear request because of its capabilities or access, so its output alone tells you little about someone's management ability. The useful mirror is the interaction: what you assume, what you explain, and how you respond when the work comes back wrong. Those are habits you bring to human teams too.

## Three Skills You Already Use

The overlap shows up in ordinary delegation. Someone needs enough context to understand the problem, feedback they can act on, and a clear idea of the result you need. Working with AI gives you plenty of chances to notice when one of those is missing.

**1. Providing Context vs. Giving Vague Instructions**:
Suppose you ask a colleague, "Can you look into our churn problem?" They have to guess which customers matter, what period to examine, and what you expect back. You may already know all of that, which makes the omissions difficult to notice.

A more useful brief might be: "Churn among mid-market accounts rose this quarter. Review their support tickets and cancellation feedback from the last 90 days. Bring back the recurring problems and the evidence behind them so we can choose what to investigate next."

The same missing context can send an AI analysis in the wrong direction. If you supply the relevant records, customer segment, and decision the analysis should support, you give it a defined problem to work on. Writing that brief also makes you decide what you actually need.

**2. Iterating with Feedback vs. Micromanaging the Process**:
Feedback gives someone information they didn't have in the first brief. If a product spec misses the point, saying "make it more strategic" leaves them guessing again. Explaining that the proposal ignores the buyer's approval process gives them a problem they can address.

With AI, it is easy to spend several turns replacing individual sentences while leaving the underlying misunderstanding intact. A useful revision explains what the draft got wrong and why it matters. That is also the kind of feedback a colleague needs when you disagree with their approach.

**3. Defining Outcomes vs. Assigning Tasks**:
A list of completed tasks can still leave the problem unresolved. Asking someone to collect cancellation reasons, build a dashboard, and write a report specifies activity. Explaining which decision the work should support gives them a reason to question a step that adds little.

For example: "We need to decide which onboarding problem to address this month. Use these support tickets and funnel data to compare the recurring problems, show the evidence for each, and identify what we still don't know." The intended result gives the analysis a direction. It also gives you a way to judge whether another chart or another page of text helps.

Delegating this way requires you to make the decision and its constraints explicit. You have to explain the priority, the available information, and where the other party has room to choose an approach. That work is part of leading, even when writing the brief takes longer than issuing the task.

## What Your Direction Reveals

Human colleagues often compensate for gaps in a brief. They know the customer, remember an earlier meeting, or ask someone else what you meant. That work can stay invisible to the person delegating, especially when the final result is good.

An AI interaction can bring those gaps back to you quickly. If you keep adding context that was obvious to you from the start, you get to see what handing over the work actually required. Getting better at that makes you more deliberate about how you direct people too.

## AI Reflects the Operator

A vague request, missing priority, or confused strategy can return as weak output very quickly. Working with AI makes those gaps easier to see because the system has less of the shared context a human colleague might use to fill them.

That frustration can be useful evidence about your own direction. Inspect the brief as well as the result. A tool can fail a clear requirement, but it can also expose a decision you never made.
