## The Feature Checklist Fallacy

A common path to product mediocrity begins with a seemingly harmless request: the demand for more features. It starts with a competitor comparison, a checklist from a sales prospect, or a desire to close every perceived gap. This thinking leads to bloated products, diluted value propositions, and a team perpetually trapped in a feature factory.

The underlying belief is that a better product is one that simply does more. This is a fallacy. If adding every "missing" feature was the key to success, the market would be dominated by cluttered, swiss-army-knife products that nobody loves.

A useful strategy identifies the workflows that make customers choose the product and gives them sustained attention. Additional capability should be evaluated by how it supports those workflows and what it costs to maintain.

## Identify Your Uncompromising Core

The most difficult, and most important, job in product strategy is to identify the handful of attributes that represent the uncompromising core of your product. What are the two or three things that it must do exceptionally well? These are the elements that create genuine value and differentiation. Other capabilities may still matter, but they should have a clear supporting role.

Some products need broad coverage to be usable, especially when replacing an established system. The team still needs to distinguish the minimum coverage required to participate from the strengths that make a customer prefer it. Truly great products are often unapologetically "bad" at things that don't matter to their core mission. They make opinionated trade-offs, and their strength comes from what they choose *not* to do.

## Escape the Feature Trap

This principle is visible in the DNA of many successful B2B products.

Consider [Slack](https://slack.com) in its early days. It was great at three things:
1.  **Instant, channel-based communication.** It was a fundamentally new way for teams to communicate in real-time.
2.  **Deep, powerful integrations.** It became the central notification hub for a team's entire tool stack.
3.  **A fully searchable archive.** Every conversation became a permanent, accessible piece of company knowledge.

At the same time, it was mediocre or even bad at many other things. Threaded conversations were a known weakness for years. Video calling was clunky. File management was rudimentary. Those gaps still mattered to some teams. For customers who prioritized searchable, integrated team communication, the core workflow could be useful enough to justify accepting them.

Or look at [Figma](https://figma.com). It launched into a crowded market and won by being great at:
1.  **Real-time, multiplayer collaboration.** Seeing multiple cursors moving in a single design file felt like magic.
2.  **Browser-based accessibility.** There were no installs, no version control nightmares, and no platform lock-in.
3.  **A superior vector editing engine.** Its "vector networks" were a fundamentally better way to draw, a detail that mattered deeply to its core users.

Initially, Figma's prototyping features were far less advanced than competitors, and it lacked a plugin ecosystem. Its collaborative workflow gave the target audience a reason to adopt it despite gaps elsewhere. The general lesson is to investigate which tradeoffs customers will accept for the capability they value.

## Applying the Framework

This principle is more than a historical observation; it is an active framework for making decisions. For any product you are building, you must be able to answer these questions with painful clarity:

1. Which few workflows make the strongest case for choosing our product?
2. Does our allocation of time reflect their importance?
3. Which limitations are we willing to accept, and which would prevent the target customer from succeeding?

If you cannot answer these questions, you are likely drifting toward the feature checklist trap.

## Great Requires Sacrifice

The features you leave out protect time for the few things customers should remember you for. That is a difficult trade because each rejected request can sound reasonable, and the work it displaces may already feel good enough.

A product can keep getting broader while the reason to choose it gets weaker. Focus means accepting some of those missing capabilities so the core experience can become exceptional. The team has to be willing to carry that choice through the next request.
