## The Admin Layer Is Disappearing

A product manager can spend a whole day updating tickets, collecting analytics screenshots, summarizing calls, and reconciling roadmap documents. Each task helps the organization coordinate. Together they can leave little time to decide whether the product is solving the right problem.

Agents can take on parts of that information work. They can prepare a status update, inspect a funnel, or find related feedback, provided they have access and enough context. This gives product teams a chance to spend more time interpreting what happened and choosing what to do next.

That chance only matters if the team changes how it works. Producing more tickets and reports can keep everyone busy without improving a single decision.

## The Loop Beats the Roadmap

Strategy, roadmap, specs, tickets, and dashboards describe different parts of the same product. They become difficult to use when each lives in a separate tool and changes on a different schedule. The team then spends its review meeting trying to recover why something was built.

I think the useful unit is the loop connecting those artifacts:

1. Define the strategy.
2. Turn it into specific work.
3. Ship the work.
4. Read what happened.
5. Feed the learning back into the strategy.

An agent helping with any step needs the relevant decisions from the others. If that context is scattered across chats and people's memories, the agent may guess or repeatedly ask for help. Making the links explicit helps the human team for the same reason.

## Strategy Becomes Context

A strategy that says “improve activation” leaves too much open for someone choosing the next task. Which users are struggling, at which moment, and why does that matter to the business? Those choices need to be available when the team or an agent proposes work.

A useful strategy records:

- **The target problem:** The painful, recurring problem being addressed
- **The user:** Who experiences it and in what circumstances
- **The approach:** The specific angle the team is taking
- **The metrics:** What would indicate that people are getting value
- **The boundaries:** Work the team is deliberately excluding for now

These constraints help filter proposals without requiring a new strategy meeting for every decision. Keep them current and trace changes to evidence. A stale strategy can direct an agent confidently toward work the team has already abandoned.

## Tickets Are Not Thinking

I used to think detailed tickets were a sign of good product management. Background, user story, acceptance criteria, analytics, and rollout plan all made the process feel professional. They can also make an unresolved idea feel ready before anyone has checked whether the problem is worth solving.

Use conversation and exploration before committing to the execution format. For onboarding, an agent could compare feedback, funnel behavior, and recent changes, then propose several explanations for the drop-off. The team can challenge those explanations before choosing a feature.

Once the decision is made, a ticket is useful for recording behavior, constraints, and verification. Its completeness should reflect the thinking that happened, rather than conceal the thinking that remains.

## The Product Pulse

Dashboards accumulate easily. A team can track activation, retention, conversion, revenue, support, and performance while still struggling to explain what deserves attention this week.

A product pulse is a short recurring readout that connects those signals to decisions. It should answer:

- Did users reach the moments of value we care about?
- Did metrics tied to our strategy move?
- Did anything break technically?
- Did a segment behave differently than expected?
- What should we investigate next?

Agents can help collect and compare the evidence across analytics, logs, payments, and support. Keep source references and distinguish observations from explanations. A confident summary is more useful when someone can inspect how it reached its claims.

Save the readouts with the decisions they informed. Over time, this gives the team a way to see when a pattern started and whether its response changed anything.

## Keep Talking to Users

A funnel can show where people leave without explaining why. A support summary can group complaints while losing the detail that makes one of them important. Direct conversations expose constraints that the existing measurement system may never have captured.

Faster implementation makes this contact worth protecting. A shallow assumption can become working software before anyone has questioned it. Use agents to prepare interview guides, locate relevant feedback, and connect quotes to metrics, while continuing to hear how people describe the work themselves.

## Operating the Product System

The product leader maintains the connection between strategy, context, evidence, and decisions. That includes deciding which metrics matter, who owns an investigation, and when new evidence should change the plan.

The role requires enough precision for someone else to act and enough skepticism to question a polished answer. It also requires restraint: the system can generate an unlimited amount of proposed work. The team has to choose which of it deserves attention.

## Design the Learning Loop

Agents make weak product management more expensive. A vague strategy supplies vague direction. Vanity metrics make noise look like progress. A broken feedback loop lets the team ship the next feature before it has understood the last one.

I think the useful change in the role is taking responsibility for that whole loop. Strategy has to reach implementation, and the result has to reach the next decision. Faster information work creates an opportunity to close that distance; producing more tickets leaves it open.
