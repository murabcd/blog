## The Meeting Where Opinions Compete

You have a strong view about what the product needs next. Then someone more senior disagrees, and the conversation turns toward their opinion. Your idea may be good, but the team has little to compare beyond two people's confidence. Authority settles what the argument couldn't.

This is where a product manager has more work to do. Intuition helps you notice something worth pursuing; building a case gives other people a reason to pursue it with you. I think that second part gets underestimated. A useful idea can go nowhere if you cannot explain what you saw and why it deserves the team's time.

## Turn a Feeling into a Hypothesis

Suppose you think onboarding asks too much of a new customer. That can mean several things: too many fields, unclear instructions, missing permissions, or a setup task that requires a colleague. Each explanation suggests a different change.

The instinct is useful because it points you toward a problem. Getting specific about the cause gives the team something to discuss beyond whether they share your feeling. Someone can challenge the explanation, bring a missing customer example, or suggest a cheaper way to address it. The idea gets stronger through that conversation.

## Use Evidence That Can Change the Decision

Useful evidence can come from several places:

- **Usage:** Where people leave, retry, or ask for help
- **Conversations:** How customers describe the task and its constraints
- **Support:** Repeated failures and the workarounds people use
- **Sales:** Conditions that affect buying decisions for the target customer

The case gets stronger when those sources connect. If people abandon a setup step and support repeatedly has to explain that same step, you can show the team a pattern they can investigate. A lost deal or one frustrated customer deserves attention, but a single story can give almost any opinion the appearance of evidence.

This also changes the conversation with someone more senior. You have something specific to examine together: the behavior, its consequences, and the reason you think a change would help. Their experience can improve the case without becoming the only reason for the decision.

## Build a Case That Can Be Challenged

An analytics partner can help long before you need a dashboard. Bring them the customer problem and the explanation you're considering. They can help you find out whether the pattern exists, how many people it affects, and what your initial reading missed. That is a much better conversation than requesting a chart to decorate a decision you've already made.

The same applies when discussing an idea with the team. A claim that a feature will improve retention becomes easier to assess when you can explain which customer behavior should change first. People can disagree with that reasoning and help refine it. They can also see where your confidence comes from.

The goal is a decision the team can inspect. Winning the meeting is a weak result if the argument cannot survive contact with the customer.

## Arm Your Intuition

Your intuition earns weight when people can follow it from an observation to a decision and see what happened afterward. Over time, that gives your product sense something better to work with than the memory of the arguments you won.

It also builds trust. Colleagues learn that you bring reasons they can examine and that you will revisit them when the result surprises you. Your judgment starts to carry weight beyond your title, because people have seen how you arrive at it.
