## Look at the Work Behind the Role

A product role can keep its title while the work changes around it. Agents may take on research preparation, drafting, or implementation, leaving the person with more responsibility for choosing the problem and checking the result. A career plan built around yesterday's task list can miss that change.

When people ask me about career choices, I would start with the work they will be allowed to own. The company's stage and reputation matter, but so do the manager, the customer access, and the decisions that actually reach the role.

## Understand the Playing Field

AI creates opportunities in new products and changes how established teams operate. Some companies will capture substantial value; others will spend heavily without finding a durable business. A category's popularity is weak evidence about the quality of a particular job.

Look for a clear customer problem, a credible way to reach those customers, and a team that can explain its economics. Ask how AI changes the product and the operation. The answer should describe actual work rather than depend on the company being part of a trend.

## Choose Your Arena Wisely

An early-stage startup can give you broad ownership and direct contact with customers. It also carries uncertainty about funding, product-market fit, and whether the role will still exist in its current form. A large company may offer resources and experienced colleagues, while narrowing the decisions you can make.

Growth-stage companies can be attractive because there is a business to scale and enough unfinished organization to create scope. Series C through Series E is a useful area to investigate, but a funding label does not guarantee product-market fit, sound finances, or a good manager.

I would compare actual responsibilities, runway, team quality, and learning opportunities across offers. Choose the tradeoff that fits your financial position and the kind of work you want to become good at.

## Build Judgment through Delivery

Clarity helps people trust your work. Learn to break an ambiguous problem into assumptions, evidence, constraints, and a decision. Then explain it well enough that another person can act without reconstructing your thinking.

Product judgment develops through repeated contact with results. Study useful products, build things, observe what people do, and revisit your predictions. Shipping gives you evidence, especially when you remain responsible for what happens after release.

Take risks with an identifiable upside and a manageable downside. A messy project can teach you a lot when you have access, support, and a real decision to make. Permanent chaos without authority can consume time without building the experience you expected.

## Build Relationships through Useful Work

Professional relationships grow when people see how you think and know they can depend on you. Help with a problem you understand, share relevant knowledge, or make an introduction when there is a reason for it.

Be generous within limits you can sustain. Helping someone should not require an immediate exchange, but it also should not become an obligation to take on work you cannot do well. Over time, useful collaboration gives people a more accurate picture of your strengths than a polished introduction does.

## Choose the Learning You Can Point To

Before taking a role, describe what you expect to be able to do better a year later. Then check whether the job gives you the authority, feedback, and support to learn it.

That makes the decision more concrete than choosing the company that sounds closest to the future. You are committing time to an environment; make sure the work inside it can produce the experience you want.
