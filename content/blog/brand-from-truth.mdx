## Most Branding Starts in the Wrong Place

The normal way people approach branding is to ask some version of the same question: how do we want people to see us? It sounds strategic. It makes people think about positioning, aspiration, tone, and category. But it usually pushes teams into performance too early.

They start inventing a version of the company they hope the market will admire before dealing with the company that actually exists. That is where a lot of weak branding comes from.

## You Can Only Pull Off What Is True

Start by figuring out who you actually are. What do you do better than other people? What kind of customer do you understand? What kind of energy does the company naturally have?

Are you sharp and opinionated? Quiet and trustworthy? Technical and precise? Fast and scrappy? Those choices matter because the company has to keep inhabiting the identity after the branding exercise ends.

If the brand requires everyone to act like someone else, it will fall apart the moment real work begins.

## Marketing Breaks When the Company Is Pretending

This is why some marketing feels off even when it looks polished. The copy is clean. The visuals are good. The positioning sounds expensive. But something does not hold together.

The product feels different from the promise. The founder sounds different from the website. The sales call sounds different from the ads. The customer experience sounds different from all of it.

People often send this back to the copywriter. The identity underneath the copy deserves the same attention.

## The Market Can Feel the Mismatch

Customers may not describe the problem cleanly, but they encounter it whenever the company borrows a personality it cannot sustain. They have to work out which version they will get after signing.

A startup that is fast and technical should be careful about sounding like a polished enterprise incumbent. A company that wins on trust and depth gains little from forcing itself into a loud growth voice. An understated founder can explain the work well without cosplaying a Twitter main character.

The market needs a coherent identity it can recognize across those encounters. A perfectly manufactured one can make the mismatch worse.

## Strong Brands Usually Get Narrower

Once you start from truth, the brand usually gets sharper. You stop collecting generic traits like “innovative,” “customer-centric,” or “world-class.” You stop writing positioning that could belong to ten other startups in the same category.

Instead, you begin to define the company around a few things that are real. That can feel less ambitious in the room because it gives up some possibilities. It also gives the right customer a clearer reason to choose you.

## Truth Has Tradeoffs

Teams avoid this because truth forces exclusion. You give up some audiences. You stop pretending you can win every conversation. You accept that some people will dislike the company because it has stopped mirroring whatever the room wants to hear.

That makes people uncomfortable, especially in marketing, where there is constant pressure to widen the funnel and soften sharp edges. Keep doing that and the position becomes agreeable enough to mean very little. A clear identity gives people something they can recognize and decide about.

## Start with Reality

If I were doing this exercise with a team, I would begin with questions about the work:

- Why do customers actually choose us today?
- Which parts of the company feel natural and repeatable?
- Where do we consistently overperform?
- What tone feels true when the founder, product, and team are at their best?
- What promises can we make without acting?

That is where the material is. Branding compresses the truth of the company into something the market can understand. The team can choose what to emphasize once it knows what it has to work with.

## Reveal What Is Already There

You can polish the edges, improve the storytelling, and choose what to emphasize. But the raw material still matters. If the company does not know who it is, a more expensive presentation will leave the same question unanswered.

The strongest brand gives a company a recognizable expression of something it can keep being. People should be able to encounter the product, the founder, or the support team and feel that they have met the same company.
