## The Pressure to Reduce Churn

For many product teams, the directive to "reduce churn" is a constant source of pressure and frustration. It triggers a predictable, and often flawed, response. We hastily build steep off-boarding discounts, add "pause your subscription" options, and generally do whatever it takes to stop a user from clicking the final "cancel" button.

This approach is rooted in a fundamental misunderstanding of the problem. We treat churn as the disease, when in reality, it's just the final, lagging symptom of a much deeper issue. Chasing churned users is a reactive, low-leverage game.

Start with the customers the product is meant to serve and investigate why they leave. Renaming churn as retention changes little by itself; the useful change is which customer problem receives attention.

## The Myth of Zero Churn

The initial impulse when tackling churn is to treat every lost customer as an equal failure. This leads to a frantic effort to be everything to everyone, which is a recipe for a mediocre product.

The truth is, not all churn is created equal. A critical first step is to distinguish between **regrettable churn** and **unregrettable churn**.

- **Unregrettable churn** comes from "bad-fit" customers. These are users who are outside your ICP (ideal customer profile). They signed up for your product hoping it would solve a problem you were never designed to address. Trying to retain these users is a waste of resources. It pulls your roadmap in the wrong direction and pollutes your feedback with irrelevant requests. You should let them go gracefully.

- **Regrettable churn** is the one that matters. This is when a customer within your ICP leaves because the product or its delivery failed to meet an important need. This is the churn that signals a true problem with your product, onboarding, or overall experience.

Separate these groups before choosing a response. Churn outside the ICP can still expose misleading acquisition or positioning, while churn within it deserves investigation into fit, delivery, price, and the customer’s changing circumstances.

## The Resurrection Fallacy

The next common trap is the focus on resurrection. We spend enormous effort trying to win back customers who have already left, often with short-term incentives that do little to address the root cause of their departure.

This is the "resurrection fallacy." It's an expensive and often futile exercise for a simple reason: a resurrected user is not the same as a new user. They have already given your product a chance and found it lacking. A discount does not resolve the reason they left. A return can make sense when the product, the customer’s situation, or the original failure has materially changed.

A far more effective strategy is to re-frame the problem from resurrection to retention. Prioritize the recurring causes of departure over incentives that leave those causes untouched. This means investing your resources where they have the most impact: preventing your ideal customers from becoming dissatisfied in the first place.

## Churn Is a Lagging Indicator

Many teams measure churn as a binary state: an account is active or it has left. This leads them to focus all their energy on that final moment of cancellation.

Cancellation can follow weeks of friction, but it can also follow a budget cut, a merger, or a completed project. Inspect the path rather than assuming every departure has the same cause. Earlier signs of product trouble give the team more time to respond.

The real work is in monitoring the *leading* indicators that predict it. These are found throughout the entire customer lifecycle:

- **Onboarding:** Did the user successfully complete the key setup steps?
- **Activation:** Did they experience the "aha!" moment and the core value of your product?
- **Engagement:** Is their usage of key features healthy and consistent, or is it declining over time?

By focusing on these upstream metrics, you shift from a reactive to a proactive stance. You solve problems before they become reasons to leave.

## Fix What Happens before Churn

A cancellation flow sees the end of the relationship. The product team needs to understand the work that came before it: whether setup succeeded, whether the customer reached value, and what changed afterward.

That gives the next investment a reason. Fix an onboarding failure, strengthen a recurring use case, or stop attracting a segment the product cannot serve. Another discount can postpone the departure while leaving all three decisions unresolved.
